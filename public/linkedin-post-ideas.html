50 LinkedIn post ideas you can write this week.

Running out of things to post usually means looking in the wrong place. These ideas pull posts out of your own work, mistakes and opinions, whatever your industry.

By David Tost, maker of Sona. Published 15 November 2026.

Running out of things to post is rarely about having nothing to say. It’s usually about looking in the wrong place. Your work, your mistakes and your opinions are full of posts, as long as you know which questions to ask.

Below are 50 LinkedIn post ideas in six groups. Each one works for any industry, because the material comes from you. Pick one, add a specific detail only you could know, and you have a post.

50 LinkedIn post ideas

From your work

  • The decision you made this month that you’d make differently now.
  • A process you use every week, written out step by step.
  • The question clients or colleagues ask you most, answered properly.
  • A tool you stopped using, and what replaced it.
  • What a normal day in your job actually looks like, hour by hour.
  • The mistake new people in your role make in their first month.
  • Something you measured, and what the numbers showed.
  • A before-and-after of a piece of your work, with what changed.
  • The checklist you run before you ship, send or sign anything.
  • What you’d tell yourself on your first day in this job.

Stories

  • The moment you nearly quit, and what kept you going.
  • A conversation that changed how you work, told in a few lines.
  • Your first sale, first client or first paycheck in your field.
  • A failure with a specific cost: money, time or a client.
  • Someone who helped you early on, and exactly what they did.
  • An email or message you’ll never forget, quoted word for word if you can.
  • A rule you broke that turned out well.
  • The project that taught you the most, even if nobody saw it.

Opinions

  • Advice everyone in your industry repeats that you disagree with.
  • A trend you think is overrated, and what you’d do instead.
  • Something you changed your mind about, and why.
  • The skill you think is underrated in your field.
  • A prediction about your industry for the next two years.
  • What you’d remove from your industry’s standard way of working.
  • A common metric you think measures the wrong thing.

Teaching

  • A how-to for one small task, in five steps or fewer.
  • Three common terms in your field, explained in plain words.
  • A template you use, shared in full.
  • The resources that taught you the most: books, people or courses.
  • A breakdown of something that worked for someone else, and why it worked.
  • Two approaches to the same problem, compared honestly.
  • The first three things you’d do in a new role like yours.
  • A short glossary of jargon newcomers find confusing.

Behind the scenes

  • What you’re working on this week, and the hardest part of it.
  • The real numbers behind a launch: what went well and what didn’t.
  • How you plan your week, with a photo or screenshot of the plan.
  • A decision your team argued about, and how you settled it.
  • What you spend money on in your business, and what you cut.
  • Your setup: the tools and habits that make your day work.
  • A goal you set at the start of the year, and an honest progress check.

Conversation starters

  • A question you genuinely want answers to from people in your field.
  • Shine a light on someone else’s work and say what you learned from it.
  • Ask for recommendations, then share the best answers in a follow-up post.
  • Share a small win from your network, with their permission.
  • A poll with two real options you’re choosing between.
  • Thank someone publicly for a specific thing they did.
  • Ask people for the worst advice they were ever given.
  • Respond to a post that made you think, adding your own angle.
  • Share a lesson from a comment someone left on your post.
  • A “what would you do?” situation from your own work.

How to turn an idea into a post

  1. Add one specific detail. A number, a date, a name or a quote. “A tool I stopped using” becomes “I cancelled a €99-a-month tool last week. Nobody on the team noticed.”
  2. Write the hook last. Draft the post first, then write three openings and keep the strongest. Our list of 30 LinkedIn hook examples shows six patterns to try.
  3. Keep one idea per post. If you find two, you have next week’s post too.
  4. End with something to do or answer. A takeaway or a real question gives people a reason to reply.

Which ideas tend to do best

That depends on your niche, and the only reliable way to know is to look. Find posts in your field that did far better than their author usually does, and note which kind of idea they were: a story, an opinion, a how-to. Our guide to finding viral LinkedIn posts in your niche shows how to do that by hand.

A simple weekly plan

  • Monday: something from your work (a process, a decision, a number).
  • Wednesday: a story or an opinion.
  • Friday: a conversation starter, then spend ten minutes replying to comments.

Three posts a week is plenty. One good post beats five rushed ones.

Questions people ask

What should I post on LinkedIn?
Things only you can write: decisions you made, mistakes with a real cost, processes you use, opinions on your industry and questions you genuinely want answered. Add one specific detail to each.
How often should I post on LinkedIn?
Two or three times a week is enough for most people. Consistency matters, but a specific, useful post matters more than posting every day.
What kind of LinkedIn posts get the most engagement?
It varies by niche. Stories with real detail, clear opinions and practical how-tos tend to do well. Check which posts did best for creators in your own field before deciding.
How do I come up with LinkedIn post ideas every week?
Keep a running note. Whenever a client asks a question, you make a decision or something goes wrong, write one line down. Each line is a future post.
