30 LinkedIn hooks, sorted by pattern.

The first two lines decide whether anyone reads your post. Here are 30 example openings in six patterns, and the rules behind the ones that work.

By David Tost, maker of Sona. Published 15 November 2026.

On LinkedIn, most people only see the first two or three lines of your post before the “see more” cut. That opening is the hook. If it doesn’t earn the click, the rest of the post might as well not exist.

Below are 30 LinkedIn hook examples, sorted into six patterns, followed by the rules that make them work and the mistakes that sink most openings. Every example here was written for this guide to show a pattern; none are quotes from real posts, so use the shape and bring your own story.

What a LinkedIn hook is

A hook is the opening of a post: the line or two that decide whether someone expands it. A good hook does one of three things: it creates tension (“this went the opposite way”), it makes a specific promise (“7 questions, one saved me twice”), or it starts a story in the middle of the action.

30 LinkedIn hook examples, by pattern

The reversal

Starts from something the reader believes, then shows the opposite result.

  • I stopped posting every day. My reach went up.
  • The best hire I ever made had no experience in our industry.
  • We raised our prices by 40%. We lost two clients and gained eleven.
  • Networking events never got me a single client. Replying to comments did.
  • I deleted our onboarding call. Customers stayed longer.

The specific story

One moment with a time, a number or a quote. Detail makes it believable.

  • At 9:40 on a Monday my biggest client emailed one line: “We’re pausing.”
  • My first invoice was for €150. I was too nervous to send it for three days.
  • In 2019 I was fired over a Slack message. Here’s what it said.
  • A stranger left one comment on my post last month. It changed how I price everything.
  • I spent my last €2,000 on a course. The only useful part was one sentence.

The list with a promise

Says how many, what for, and gives a reason to read to the middle.

  • 7 questions I ask before every hire. Number 4 has saved me twice.
  • 5 lines I cut from every first draft. The post always gets better.
  • 3 emails that got me meetings with people who never reply.
  • 9 tools I pay for. Only 2 of them I’d buy again.
  • 4 things I check before I sign any contract. It takes ten minutes.

The confession

An honest admission earns attention, as long as the post pays it back with a lesson.

  • I sent 400 cold emails last quarter. Here’s the embarrassing reason only 3 got replies.
  • For two years I pretended to understand our numbers. I didn’t.
  • I’ve rewritten this post six times because I’m scared of how it sounds.
  • My most successful product started as a joke I almost didn’t ship.
  • I lost our best client because of a habit I still have.

The sharp opinion

A clear position people want to agree or argue with, which fills the comments.

  • Most “personal brand” advice is just posting more. That’s why it doesn’t work.
  • Your job title is the least interesting thing on your profile.
  • If your post needs a hashtag to be found, the first line isn’t doing its job.
  • Meetings without a written agenda should be cancelled. Every time.
  • Being “consistent” is overrated. Being specific isn’t.

The question

Easy to answer from experience, so readers reply. Best when you answer it yourself first.

  • What’s one thing you believed at 22 that turned out to be wrong?
  • Would you take a 20% pay cut for a four-day week?
  • Which skill have you paid for that you wish you’d learned for free?
  • How many of your best clients came from a cold message?
  • What’s the worst advice you were given early in your career?

Four rules for writing a hook that works

  1. Be specific. “I lost a client” is forgettable. “At 9:40 on a Monday my biggest client emailed one line” is not. Numbers, times and quotes do the work.
  2. Keep it short. The first line should fit on a phone screen without wrapping more than once. Put a line break after it.
  3. Open a loop you will close. Promise something the post actually delivers. A hook that over-promises gets the click once and loses the reader for good.
  4. Write it last. Draft the post first, then write three different hooks and keep the one you would stop for.

Hook mistakes to avoid

  • Warming up. “I’ve been thinking a lot lately about…” spends the most valuable line on nothing.
  • Bait you can’t back up. “This will change your life” trains people to ignore you.
  • Starting with a hashtag or a link. Both push the actual idea below the cut.
  • Copying a hook word for word. Readers notice, and it never sounds like you. Borrow the pattern instead.

Questions people ask

What is a hook on LinkedIn?
The first line or two of a post, shown before the “see more” cut. Its only job is to make the right people want to read the rest.
How long should a LinkedIn hook be?
Short enough to read at a glance on a phone: usually one sentence of under about 15 words, followed by a line break.
What makes a good LinkedIn hook?
Specific detail, a bit of tension and a promise the post keeps. Reversals, short stories, lists with a clear payoff, confessions, sharp opinions and real questions all work.
Can I reuse LinkedIn hook templates?
Yes, as patterns. Keep the structure and fill it with your own numbers and experience. Copying someone’s exact words rarely works and doesn’t sound like you.
